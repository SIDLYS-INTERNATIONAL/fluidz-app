How to remove GPS and hidden data from your photos before you share them

Your photos can carry your location, your phone model and the exact time. Here’s how to check, and how to strip it on iPhone, Android, Mac and Windows.

A photo is more than a picture. Behind the pixels, your phone quietly attaches a small label to the file — and if you send the file as it is, the label travels with it. This guide shows you how to check what yours says and how to wipe it, in a couple of minutes. No drama, no jargon: just a good habit when you share photos with people you haven’t met yet.

Quick answer

Before you send a photo to someone you don’t know, check whether it carries a location, then remove it: on iPhone, Share → Options → Location off; on Mac, Preview’s Inspector; on Windows, Properties → Details; on Android, edit the location in Google Photos. Or send a screenshot. And remember: even a clean file still shows everything visible in the picture.

What’s hiding inside a photo

Most phone and camera photos carry a block of hidden information called metadata (the technical name is EXIF). It can include:

Location

GPS coordinates, sometimes accurate to a few metres — enough to point at your home, your gym or your hotel.

Phone or camera

The make and model of the device that took the picture.

Date and time

The exact moment the photo was taken, down to the second.

Software

Sometimes the app or editing tool that touched the file.

Why it matters: when you share photos with strangers, you may be handing over more than you meant to. A photo taken at home and sent with its location can show where you live; a few timestamps can show when you’re usually there. Most of the time nothing happens — but you can’t take a file back once it’s sent, so it’s a cheap habit to build.

Don’t rely on an app to strip it for you. What a chat or dating app does with your photo’s hidden data depends on the app, and it can change. Check the file you send, not the app you send it with.

How to check what a photo reveals

Each system has a quick way to look. If you see a map, an address or GPS coordinates, the photo carries a location. (Menu names vary by version.)

iPhone

Open the photo in Photos, then swipe up (or tap the info button). If the photo has a location, you’ll see a map.

Mac

Open the photo in Preview, then Tools → Show Inspector and look for the GPS tab. No GPS tab usually means no location.

Windows

Right-click the file → Properties → Details, and scroll to the GPS section for latitude and longitude.

Android

Open the photo in Google Photos and swipe up for the details. A map or an address means a location is stored.

How to remove it

Work on a copy and send the clean one. (Menu names vary by version.)

iPhone

Open the photo, tap Share, then Options at the top of the sheet, switch Location off and send. Or skip all this and send a screenshot.

Android

In Google Photos, open the photo, swipe up, tap the edit (pencil) icon next to the location and choose to remove it. Or send a screenshot.

Mac

Preview → Tools → Show Inspector → GPS, then Remove Location Info. Save, ideally under a new name.

Windows

Right-click → Properties → Details, then Remove Properties and Personal Information. Choose to create a copy with all possible properties removed.

The screenshot trick

A screenshot is a brand-new image, so it doesn’t carry the original photo’s location. It’s the fastest fix. Two caveats: you lose a bit of quality, and a screenshot still shows everything on screen — so crop out the clock, notifications and anything with a name on it.

What gives you away without any data

Removing hidden data does nothing about what’s visible. Take a second look at the picture itself — only if you want to stay anonymous, of course; that’s your call:

  • Reflections in mirrors, windows, glasses or a phone screen
  • The view from a window or balcony
  • Street signs, house numbers and shop fronts
  • A work badge, lanyard or uniform
  • A unique tattoo or scar
  • A recognisable room, wall art or building

Before you send: a 30-second checklist

  • Check the file for a location, and remove it.
  • Scan the picture: reflections, window view, signs, badges.
  • Crop or blur anything you’d rather keep private.
  • Send the clean copy, and keep the original for yourself.
  • Not sure? Send a screenshot instead.

Optional: a visible date stamp

Some people add a small, visible date to a photo to show it was taken recently — a simple way to say “yes, this is me, now”. Unlike hidden data, it’s something you choose and everyone can see.

Quick questions

Does removing the data make my photo look worse?

No. Removing hidden data doesn’t touch the pixels, so the photo looks exactly the same. Only the screenshot route can lower the quality.

Is a screenshot always safe?

It doesn’t carry the original file’s location, but it shows everything on your screen — the time, notifications, names. Crop it and look it over before you send.

Do I need to do this for every photo?

It’s a good default for anyone you haven’t met. For friends it’s your call. Either way, remember that a photo can be forwarded or saved once you’ve sent it.
