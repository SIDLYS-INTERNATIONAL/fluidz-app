A hookup safety checklist: before, during and after

A practical, non-judgy checklist for meeting someone from an app: verify him, tell a friend, set your limits, check in, and look after yourself afterwards.

Meeting someone from an app can be exciting, easy and completely fine — and most of the time, that’s exactly how it goes. This checklist isn’t about fear or shame. It’s a handful of good habits that take a few minutes and let you relax and enjoy yourself. Take what’s useful, skip what isn’t.

General information, not medical or legal advice. For health questions talk to a sexual-health professional; for legal ones, a qualified person where you live.
The short version

Check he’s real, tell a friend where you’ll be, meet somewhere public first, know your limits, sort out protection, and check in afterwards. Everything below is just the detail.

Before: set it up

  • Check he’s real. Ask for a short voice note or a quick video call. Do the photos match, and are they recent? Someone genuine is usually happy to do a 30-second call; endless excuses are worth a second thought.
  • Look him up. A name or username, a social profile, a mutual friend. If a friend might know him, ask discreetly.
  • Tell a friend. Share where, when and who, and turn on live location sharing with them for the evening (most phones and messaging apps have it).
  • Choose the place. Meet somewhere public first, or somewhere you know and can leave easily.
  • Decide your limits. What you’re into, what you’re not, what’s a hard no. It’s easier to settle that before than in the moment — and it’s fine to tell him upfront.
  • Sort protection and testing. Condoms and lube are the basics; PrEP and PEP exist too. Talk to a sexual-health professional about what suits you. In France, free testing is available at a CeGIDD; elsewhere, look for your local sexual-health clinic.

During: stay in charge

  • Consent is ongoing. A yes can become a no at any moment, for you and for him. Check in, listen, and stop when asked.
  • Trust your gut. If something feels off, you can leave. You don’t owe anyone an explanation.
  • Phone charged, drink in sight. Charge your phone before you go, and keep your drink where you can see it.
  • Agree a code. Pick a word or emoji with a friend that means “call me”, so you can ask for an out without saying so.

After: look after yourself

  • Check in with your friend. A quick “home safe” is enough.
  • Keep a testing rhythm. How often depends on you and your partners, so ask a sexual-health professional what’s right for you.
  • If something went wrong, you are never to blame. Talk to someone you trust. You can also contact a local LGBTQ+ health or support organisation, or the police.
  • Know your emergency number. In the EU it’s 112. If you’re in danger or need urgent medical help, call it.
If substances are involved

Plan ahead and set your limits before you go out, not in the moment. Keep a sober friend informed about where you are, and never mix substances without reliable information.

Know your local emergency number and don’t hesitate to call it. Local harm-reduction organisations offer judgment-free advice, and many are free.

Quick questions

Is it rude to ask for a video call?

Not at all. A short voice note or video call is a quick, normal way to check that someone is who they say they are. People who are genuine usually get it.

What if I can’t tell a friend?

Share what you’re comfortable with — just a time and a place — with someone you trust. If there’s truly nobody, meet somewhere public first, keep your phone charged and consider contacting a local LGBTQ+ support organisation.

How often should I get tested?

It depends on you and your partners, so there’s no single answer. A sexual-health professional can suggest a rhythm that fits; in France, a CeGIDD offers free testing.
